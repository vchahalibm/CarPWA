How DriveGen and StudioGen talk to each other

A friendly guide to the Connections part of the apps: what it does, how it keeps your messages secret, and how you stay in control. Written so a 6th grader can follow it. (Grown-ups: the detailed design is in connections.html.)

1. What it does

📱DriveGen is the app on a phone or iPad. In a car, it's your talking assistant.
💻StudioGen is the app on a Mac. It can run presentations, open apps, read data, and do jobs on the computer.

Connections lets them work as a team. From your phone you can say “run the sales summary on my Mac”, and the Mac does the job and sends the answer back to your phone, even if you're in a car far away from home.

2. The front-door problem

Every home or school network has a router. Think of it as the front door of a building. It lets people inside go out, and it lets answers come back in. But if a stranger knocks from outside, the door stays shut. That's good for safety, but it means your phone (outside, on mobile data) can't just knock on your Mac's door.

3. Meeting places (relays)

So the two apps meet somewhere both can reach: a relay. A relay is like a public bulletin board or a post office locker on the internet. Each app walks out its own front door (always allowed!), pins messages on the board, and reads the messages left for it.

The apps use free, public relays that already exist. There are three kinds, and the apps can use all of them:

MQTT brokers 📬
Message boards made for smart gadgets (lights, sensors). Fast and simple.
Nostr relays 📡
Message boards run by many different people around the world.
WebTorrent trackers 🤝
Only good for introductions (“here's how to reach me”), not for carrying your actual messages.
💡 Wait, it's public? Yes, the board is public, but your messages are inside locked boxes. Anyone can see that a box is there, but nobody can open it. That's the next part.

4. Locked boxes (how messages are kept secret)

Before a message leaves your phone or Mac, the app puts it in a locked box. This is called encryption. Only your two devices have the key that opens it.

  1. The key is a secret number made by the computer at random. It's 256 bits long, so there are more possible keys than grains of sand on all the beaches of Earth, many, many times over. Nobody can guess it.
  2. The lock is called AES-GCM. It's the same kind of lock banks and websites use. It does two jobs: it scrambles the message so it looks like noise, and it adds a seal. If anyone changes even one letter, the seal breaks and the app throws the message away.
  3. A label on the box says which way it's going (Mac → phone, or phone → Mac), and that label is part of the seal too. A box can't be turned around and sent back the other way.
  4. A timestamp and a message number go inside. Boxes older than 2 minutes, or ones the app has already seen, are thrown away. So nobody can copy a box today and send it again tomorrow to trick your Mac.
🔒 What the relay sees: a random-looking name for the box (like “sg1/7f3a9c…”), a pile of scrambled noise, how big it is, and when it was sent. What it can't see: what you said, what your Mac did, or your data.

There are two more locks for special cases:

  • Direct links (section 6) use WebRTC's own built-in lock, called DTLS, on top of everything.
  • Nostr relays need every message to carry a signature, like an autograph. The app makes its own autograph key, and it has nothing to do with your secret key.

5. Pairing: sharing the key in person

How do two devices get the same secret key without anyone else seeing it? In person, with a QR code.

  1. On the Mac: StudioGen › Settings › Connections › Pair a phone or iPad (once). A big QR code appears.
  2. On the phone: DriveGen › Settings › Connections › Pair once. Point the camera at the code.
  3. Done! The code held three things: a random room name (where to meet), the secret key, and the Mac's list of meeting places.
⚠️ Anyone who scans that QR code can pair, so only show it to your own devices, and close it when you're done.

6. Talking directly

Once they've met at a relay, the apps try to talk directly, like two friends who found each other's phone numbers. This uses a technology called WebRTC.

  1. Each app asks a STUN server, “What's my address as seen from outside?” (It's like asking someone across the street to read your house number.)
  2. They swap addresses through the relay, inside a locked box of course.
  3. Both apps send a message to each other at the same moment. Each front door sees its own app sending something out, so it lets the answer back in. This trick is called hole punching.

This works most of the time, but some front doors are extra strict (some phone companies, offices and hotels). When that happens, the relay simply keeps carrying the locked boxes. You don't have to do anything.

You can choose (on the Mac: Settings › Connections › Direct link):
  • Whenever possible: try to go direct, even between different networks.
  • Same network only: go direct only when both are on the same Wi-Fi.
  • Never: always use the relays.
Also: “Let data go over the relays when there's no direct link.” Turn it off and the relays only ever carry introductions. Then, if the doors are too strict, there's no connection, unless you add a TURN server. A TURN server is a delivery helper you set up yourself; it carries the locked traffic when nothing else can.

There's also Connect directly (scan a code each time). It works on the same Wi-Fi with no internet at all, but it doesn't remember the pairing, so you scan again next time.

7. When a meeting place closes

Public relays sometimes go offline. So the Mac keeps an ordered list of meeting places (Settings › Connections › Relays), and both apps follow the same rule:

The rule: use the first two places on the list that are working for you.
  • An app knows a place works because its own messages come back to it from there, like hearing your own echo.
  • Every “hello” (about every 15 seconds) says which places you're using, and each app also visits the other's places. That way they always meet.
  • If a place stops answering, both apps move down the list. When it comes back, they move back up.
  • If the two haven't heard each other for a while, they each check more places on the list (4, then 6) until they find each other.

The Mac is the boss of the list. You can add your own meeting place, move places up or down, and test them all. If you change the list, pair your phone again so it gets the new list. Until then, it keeps using the old one.

8. Changing the secret: you're in control

Changing your secret key now and then is good practice, like changing a password. You don't have to type anything: the Mac makes a brand-new random key for you.

How to change it (on the Mac: StudioGen › Settings › Connections):
  • Change secret next to a device: gives that device a new key now.
  • Change all secrets now: every paired device gets a new key.
  • Change every secret automatically: every day, every week, every month, or only when you ask.
  • Revoke a device: throws its key away for good. That device can't talk to your Mac any more, unless you pair it again.
  • Pair again: also makes a brand-new key (and gives the phone the newest list of meeting places).
On the phone, Settings › Connections shows when the secret last changed.

What happens when the secret changes

  1. The Mac makes a new random key and sends it to your phone, locked with the current key. So only your phone can open it.
  2. Your phone switches to the new key and replies, using the new key.
  3. When the Mac sees the new key working, it switches too.
  4. Both keep the old key for 10 minutes, only to open boxes that were already on their way. After that it's gone.
  5. If your phone was off, it gets the new key the next time it says hello.
🤔 Why not let me type my own secret word? Words people choose are much easier to guess than a random 256-bit number. A computer can try billions of common words and phrases very quickly. The random key is far stronger, and changing it often (with the button or the schedule) keeps you in control without remembering anything.
✅ We tested it: after changing the secret twice, both apps used the same new key, messages still worked, and the very first key could no longer open new messages.

9. Checking that it works

Settings › Connections › Test the connection (on both apps) checks everything in about 20 seconds and tells you in plain words:

  • What kind of front door your network has. Friendly doors keep the same address for everyone; strict ones give a new address for every place you talk to, which makes direct links harder.
  • Which meeting places work from here, and how fast each one is.
  • Whether the other app can be reached, and whether it's direct or through a relay.
  • A report you can copy and share.

The people who build the app also test it in a pretend-network lab with pretend front doors (friendly, strict, ones that block things), plus a test every night between computers in different places on the real internet.

10. Where this lives in the code

For the curious (and future programmers!). Each file is one piece of the puzzle.

FileWhat it does (in simple words)
js/relay.jsThe meeting places and the locked boxes: the list of relays (MQTT, Nostr, trackers), the rule for picking and switching places, locking and unlocking messages (AES-GCM), pairing codes, changing the secret, and the Relays and Direct link settings.
js/direct.jsTalking directly with WebRTC: making and answering the direct link, the QR codes for “Connect directly”, and reading QR codes with the camera.
js/link.jsThe front desk: picks the best way to talk (direct, then relay, then Tailscale), “run … on my Mac” by voice, the Mac's side that answers phones, and the Connections screens.
js/linktest.jsTest the connection: front-door type, every relay, the other app, and a plain-words result.
js/app.jsThe Settings page, including the Connections tab.
js/commands.jsUnderstands the voice command “run … on my Mac”.
desktop/link.jsOnly for the optional Tailscale way: a little server on the Mac and its pairing code.
desktop/main.js, desktop/preload.jsThe Mac app's engine room: starts the Tailscale server and lets the page use it, and makes the Mac share its local address in direct-link codes.
vendor/nostr/nostr.bundle.jsA helper library that signs Nostr messages (the autograph).
vendor/qrcode/qrcode.js, vendor/jsqr/jsQR.jsHelper libraries to draw QR codes and read them with the camera.
tests/net/lab.mjs, tests/net/ns.sh, tests/net/build.sh, tests/net/web.mjs, tests/net/peer/agent.mjsThe pretend-network lab: pretend front doors (friendly, strict, blocking), a pretend internet, and a robot that runs both apps and checks the results.
tests/net/remote.mjs, .github/workflows/net-nightly.ymlThe nightly test between computers in different places on the real internet.
docs/connections.html, docs/connection-tests.htmlThe grown-up versions: the full design and the test plan.
