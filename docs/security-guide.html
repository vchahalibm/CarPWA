Keys and secrets: who makes them, who holds them, who checks them

Every lock in the apps, in one place: the keys that sign updates, the keys that make your mesh yours, the fingerprint and secrets your dark servers check, the passwords on your Node-REDs, and what CamGen would need. For each: where it's made, where it lives, who uses it, and what happens if it leaks or is lost.

1. Three kinds of thing

KindWhat it isRule
secretA private key, password or shared secret. Whoever has it can do what it allows.Never leaves where it was made, except over SSH or inside a code you paste yourself.
publicA public key or a fingerprint of one. It lets anyone check a signature, but can't make one.Safe to copy, email, put in a settings file or in the app itself.
worked outMade fresh from a secret each time (today's door address, a knock link, a cookie). Short-lived on purpose.Nothing to keep: if one is seen, it stops working within minutes or a day.

Almost everything below is one of two tricks. A signature: a private key signs, and anyone holding the matching public key can check it came from that key and wasn't changed. A shared secret: two sides both hold the same secret, and each proves it knows it by mixing it with something fresh (a date, a time) through HMAC, without ever sending the secret itself.

2. Publishing: proving an update is yours

These live only on your Mac, in ~/StudioGen-update-keys/ (outside the source, never committed), or in its Keychain.

3. The mesh: proving a device is yours

When an app first joins or makes a mesh it makes its own device keys, on the device, and they never leave it: a signing key (ECDSA P-256) and a key-agreement key (ECDH P-256). The device's id is a hash of its public signing key.

  • The roster is the list of members and their public keys, signed by the owner's device. Any member can check that signature, so nobody can add themselves.
  • Pairwise keys: two devices combine their ECDH keys and each arrives at the same AES-GCM key without sending it. Everything between those two is sealed with it, whichever way it travels: direct, a public broker, the hive, or your dark relay. None of those can read it.
  • The group key is made by the owner and given to each member over its pairwise room; it seals what everyone in the mesh shares. When the owner removes someone, it changes.
  • Invites and pairing codes (SGM…, SG3…, QR codes) carry a room and a key to get a new device started. Show them only to your own devices. More in How the apps talk, including the optional secret phrase.

4. The fingerprint: pinning the servers to your mesh

Your dark servers face the internet. They let a device in if it shows a roster with that device in it, signed by the owner. But anyone can make a mesh of their own and sign a roster naming themselves as owner, and that roster would check out perfectly. So each server must be told which owner counts. That's the fingerprint: your owner's public signing key, run through SHA-256 and cut to 16 characters.

Where to see yours: NaoMuse › Settings › Connections › Dark servers, “Your mesh fingerprint” (Copy). It only changes if the mesh gets a new owner device. Then: node stack/setup.mjs --set-fingerprint <new> and naomuse-install --oracle naomuse-a1, and the installer again for home.

5. Reaching the relay, step by step

The relay carries messages between your devices when they can't reach each other directly. Two things are needed: the relay secret (it came in the SGD1… code you pasted) and your device key (it's in the app).

  1. Your device works out today's address: HMAC of the relay secret and today's date, 24 characters. Tomorrow it's a different one; the relay accepts yesterday's, today's and tomorrow's, to cover clocks and time zones.
  2. Your device opens wss://ac7j332.130-210-34-116.sslip.io/<today's address>. The name is random, so nobody finds it by guessing, and TLS hides the address from anyone watching the network.
  3. The door (Caddy, the only thing on 443) holds the TLS certificate and passes the connection to the relay inside. Any other address gets a bare 404 that names nothing.
  4. Your device speaks first, once: the owner-signed roster, plus relay|<mesh>|<device>|<time> signed with its own device key.
  5. The relay checks: is the roster's owner the fingerprint it was given? Is the owner's signature good? Is this device in it? Is its signature good, and the time within two minutes (so nobody can replay an old hello)? Any “no”: the socket closes without a word.
  6. The relay says welcome and who else of your mesh is there. From now on it forwards to → from frames between members of your mesh only. Each frame is sealed with the pair's own key, so the relay carries noise it can't read. It keeps no files and logs nothing about who talked to whom.

6. Opening a Node-RED, step by step

A browser can't sign its first message the way the app does, so NaoMuse signs for it. Each Node-RED has two locks in a row: the gate (only your devices) and Node-RED's own login.

  1. NaoMuse: you press Open beside Cloud or Home Node-RED (Settings › Connections › Dark servers). It makes a knock link: the gate's today's address (from the relay secret, as above), with the roster and gate|<mesh>|<device>|<time> signed by its device key. Good for one minute, and only once.
  2. The gate checks the same things the relay does: the fingerprint (GATE_OWNER), both signatures, the minute. Anything else, any other address, a second use: bare 404.
  3. The gate gives your browser a cookie for 12 hours, signed with the relay secret, so it remembers nothing itself. With it, everything is passed through to Node-RED, web sockets too.
  4. Node-RED asks for its login: user admin, and the password from naomuse-install --passwords (home and cloud each have their own). Credentials stored in your flows are encrypted with a separate key per Node-RED.

Home and cloud talk to each other through the link: home Node-RED dials out to the hub on Oracle (home is never reachable from outside), both prove they know the link secret, and the cloud can only call actions home has published by name in capabilities.json, with only the values each one takes. So even a taken-over Oracle machine gets only those actions.

7. The Oracle machine's own locks

  • Your SSH key ~/.ssh/naomuse-a1.key is the only way to log in (passwords and root logins are off). Its passphrase is in the Mac's Keychain and in ~/StudioGen-update-keys/oracle-ssh-passphrase.txt. Secrets reach the machine only over SSH, into ~/naomuse, which only your login can open.
  • Two firewalls, Oracle's security list and the machine's own (IPv4 and IPv6): only 22, 443, 3478 and 49160–49200 are open. The servers behind the door listen on the machine itself only.
  • TURN (3478) helps two devices on hard networks connect for calls and video; it hands out relaying only to a login made from the TURN secret, valid for minutes, and refuses private addresses as destinations so it can't be turned against your network. With CamGen's slot it takes logins from a second secret too, CamGen's own, so either can be changed without touching the other.
  • The door keeps no record of who came: no request log, and no error log (which would name each visitor's address).
The apps use it. The SGD1… code carries TURN's address and secret. Each app makes its own login from the secret, the way coturn checks it: the user is <when it ends>:<device> and the password an HMAC of it. Each login is good for 12 hours and renewed every hour. Every WebRTC link (the phone-and-Mac direct link, the mesh's direct links) then offers your TURN, and no public STUN server is asked for your address any more. Paste a new code after --rotate turn, as after --rotate relay.

10. If one leaks, or is lost

If this leaks…Someone could…Do this
Update signing keySign updates your NaoMuses would install (if they could also push to the repo)The worst one: a new key means a new app version with the new public key. Keep it backed up and private.
SGD1… code / relay secretFind today's address. Still not get in: they'd also need a device in your meshnode stack/setup.mjs --rotate relay, naomuse-install --oracle naomuse-a1 and the installer for home, then paste the new code in each app
FingerprintNothing: it's publicNothing
A Node-RED passwordNothing without also passing the gate (a device in your mesh)--rotate nodered, then install again
TURN or link secretTURN: relay traffic through your machine (it's in the code, so a lost phone counts). Link: pretend to be home or hub--rotate turn or --rotate link, install again; for TURN, paste the new code in each app
A device (phone lost)Use your mesh as that deviceOwner: remove it from the mesh (the group key changes)
SSH keyLog in to the Oracle machinestack/oracle-newkey.sh (stack/ORACLE.md, A3)

Lost rather than leaked: a lost SSH passphrase is fixed by giving the machine a new key (above). Lost dark settings (~/StudioGen-update-keys/dark/) means running setup again and pasting a new code. A lost update key means installed NaoMuses can't be updated in place, so back up ~/StudioGen-update-keys/ somewhere safe.

11. CamGen and the relay

CamGen can't use NaoMuse's relay. That relay only lets in devices of your mesh that sign with mesh keys, and CamGen isn't a mesh member; it has its own relay (CamGen/relay), with its own locks:

CamGen'sWhat it doesLike NaoMuse's
RELAY_JOIN_KEYLets a CamGen register as a site; stays with the ownerThe admin code of a hive
RELAY_SECRETMakes each site's secret and viewer key (HMAC), so the relay stores nothingThe relay secret
Viewer keyIn the site's invite code; in every viewer's address; the site's own API token still checks every requestThe SGD1… code
TURN_SECRETIn NaoMuse's slot: CamGen's own secret on NaoMuse's TURN (3478). Without the slot: its own coturn on 3479. Either way the relay hands viewers short-lived loginsNaoMuse's TURN secret (a different one)

Each app keeps its own setup and install. NaoMuse only keeps a slot for CamGen, and CamGen's own installer fills it:

  1. NaoMuse's installer: naomuse-install --camgen on, then --oracle naomuse-a1. The door gets a fourth random name leading to camgen-relay:8800 on naomuse_camgen-net (an internal network: nothing on it can reach out), and TURN a second secret. Until CamGen's relay is there, that name is a bare 404 like every other.
  2. You: naomuse-install --camgen-slot prints the CGSLOT1… code: the name, the network, and TURN's address with CamGen's secret. Treat it like a password.
  3. CamGen's installer: camgen-install --pick relay --ssh naomuse-a1 --naomuse-slot 'CGSLOT1…' makes CamGen's join key and relay secret (kept in ~/.camgen/relay), builds the relay for Arm, sends it over SSH and runs it on that network with no port of its own and no coturn of its own, then tests it from outside.
  4. CamGen: Sites › Join a relay, with the address and join key camgen-install printed. Viewers get CamGen's invite codes; their TURN logins come from CamGen's relay, for NaoMuse's TURN.

Either side can change its own TURN secret alone (--rotate camgen on NaoMuse's side, then the new code to camgen-install). naomuse-install --camgen off closes the slot; camgen-install --naomuse-slot off goes back to CamGen's own coturn on 3479.
